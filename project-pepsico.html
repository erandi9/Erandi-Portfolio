PepsiCo

Redesigning a global intranet used by 100,000 employees across multiple regions.

Role  Sr. UX Designer, PepsiCo (full time) Platform  myPepsiCo, desktop & mobile Scope  IA, cross platform design, design & dev alignment, Agile delivery

myPepsiCo is PepsiCo's global intranet, a platform used daily by roughly 100,000 employees across multiple regions to access tools, content, and internal services. I was part of the UX efforts across a full redesign: from information architecture and card sorting to cross platform design, role based experiences, and end to end delivery in a complex Agile and ServiceNow environment.

Due to company confidentiality, this case study shares a limited selection of screens and high level detail. The process and decisions are my own.
The challenge

A platform used by everyone, optimized for no one.

myPepsiCo had grown into a fragmented experience over time. Information was hard to find. Navigation didn't reflect how different employees actually thought about their work. Design patterns were inconsistent across regions, and the architecture was difficult to scale or maintain.

The challenge wasn't adding features, it was simplifying an already complex system without disrupting the workflows of 100,000 people who depended on it daily. Every structural decision had to hold up across frontline workers, desk employees, and managers, in multiple regions, on desktop and mobile, inside a platform (ServiceNow) with real technical constraints.

The goal was not a redesign for its own sake. It was a platform that worked at the scale PepsiCo actually operates at.

This one dealt with a global platform, multiple workstreams running in parallel, dozens of stakeholders, and a delivery process where design decisions had to be documented well enough for engineering teams across regions to implement consistently without ongoing hand holding.

My role

UX/UI across a multi workstream redesign

I joined as a Senior UX Designer and led key initiatives across the full redesign of myPepsiCo. That meant owning design direction, guiding a team of designers, and staying closely involved through implementation not just handing off files and stepping back.

  • —
    Led a team of designers across multiple workstreams. Guided design direction and maintained quality and consistency as the project scaled across parallel tracks.
  • —
    Defined UX strategy, information architecture, and interaction patterns. Set the structural foundation for the redesign, from nav taxonomy through component behavior.
  • —

  • —

Process

Research, structure, then screens

Before any UI work, I needed to understand how employees across very different roles and regions were actually trying to use the platform, and where the current structure was failing them.

  1. Card sorting across regions to understand how employees in different markets mentally organized the platform's content, not how the original navigation assumed they did.
  2. Navigation restructure based on sorting results, replacing the existing taxonomy with one that aligned with users' actual mental models.
  3. Role based experience design for three distinct user types, frontline workers, desk employees, and managers, each with different access needs, workflows, and device preferences.
  4. Cross platform design ensuring the experience held up on desktop and mobile, respecting the constraints of ServiceNow as the underlying platform.
  5. Agile delivery in close collaboration with engineering: 30+ user stories per sprint, structured review processes, real time unblocking, and bug tracking in Azure DevOps.
Key decisions

Simplification as the strategy

The temptation in a large enterprise redesign is to try to solve everything at once. The decisions that mattered most on this project were the ones that held the line on simplicity.

  • —
    Card sorting to redefine navigation, not gut instinct. We ran card sorting exercises to discover how employees across regions actually grouped content. The resulting taxonomy replaced assumptions with evidence, and the navigation became something people could actually find their way around.
  • —
    Simplification over feature expansion. At enterprise scale, adding features is easy. Removing steps is hard. We prioritized reducing cognitive load over launching new capabilities, the click path reduction from 14 to 6 steps came from that principle.
  • —
    Role based experiences, not one size fits all. Frontline workers, desk employees, and managers all use myPepsiCo differently. We designed distinct experiences for each, reflecting different access levels, tasks, and device realities, rather than forcing everyone into the same flow.
  • —
    Working within ServiceNow, not against it. ServiceNow is a powerful but constrained platform. Rather than designing an ideal state that engineering couldn't ship, we found the highest quality experience possible within the platform's real boundaries.

Execution & delivery

Design doesn't end at handoff

One of the biggest gaps in enterprise UX is the distance between what gets designed and what actually ships. I closed that gap by staying involved through the full Agile delivery lifecycle, not as a reviewer at the end, but as an active participant throughout.

How I bridged design and development

  • —
    Owned 30+ user stories per sprint. Created, reviewed, and approved each story to ensure design intent translated clearly into actionable engineering requirements, no ambiguity, no room for interpretation in the wrong direction.
  • —
    Established a structured story review process. Built a repeatable quality check that identified gaps before engineering picked up work, reducing back and forth mid sprint.
  • —
    Primary design contact for developers. Answered questions, clarified requirements, and unblocked progress in real time, keeping development moving without requiring engineers to hunt for answers across teams.
  • —
    Tracked bugs and implementation drift in Azure DevOps. Raised and documented issues during implementation, ensuring what shipped matched Figma designs rather than drifting through the development process.
Why this matters

At 100,000 users across multiple regions, an implementation error isn't a small thing. The discipline of closing the design to development gap, through clear story writing, structured review, and active bug tracking, is what separates a redesign that holds up in production from one that erodes over time.

What I took away

Enterprise UX is as much about alignment as it is about design

Designing for 100,000 people across multiple regions and platforms taught me that the hardest part of enterprise UX isn't the craft, it's the coordination. Getting a decision made, documented clearly enough to survive handoff, and implemented consistently across a complex engineering environment requires a different set of muscles than designing a great screen.

The click path reduction from 14 to 6 steps didn't come from a single design insight. It came from months of card sorting, stakeholder alignment, architecture decisions, story writing, developer conversations, and bug tracking, all of it adding up to something that actually shipped the way it was designed.

The principle I carry forward

A design that doesn't survive implementation isn't finished. At any scale, whether it's a concept project or a global enterprise platform, the designer's job isn't done until what was designed is what shipped.
